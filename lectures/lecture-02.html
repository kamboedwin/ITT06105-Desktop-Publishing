Getting started: one notice, three design choices

Imagine seeing a workshop announcement on a noticeboard or in a class WhatsApp group. What would you look for? Usually you want to know what the event is, when it happens and where to go. You may notice a photograph or symbol first, but you need readable words to understand the message.

This lecture explains three elements that work together in almost every publication: images show or illustrate the subject, text communicates the details, and colour helps readers identify and organise information. A good design makes these parts work together instead of competing for attention.

Our example throughout: Tumaini Community Learning Centre needs a simple notice advertising a Basic Computer Skills Workshop. We will use this example to explain why some graphic, typography and colour choices are more helpful than others.

After studying the lecture, you should be able to distinguish raster from vector graphics, select common image formats, arrange readable text, choose a suitable colour palette and improve a crowded notice. You do not need previous experience with Photoshop, Illustrator or InDesign.

1. Choose graphics that suit the message

A graphic is any visual element used in a publication, including a photograph, logo, screenshot, icon or illustration. Two pictures may look similar on a screen but be stored very differently. The two main types are raster and vector graphics.

1.1 Raster graphics: pictures made from pixels

A raster image, sometimes called a bitmap, is made from a grid of tiny coloured squares called pixels. Think of a mosaic: when you stand back, many small tiles appear to form a single picture. A digital photograph works in a similar way. Each pixel stores a colour, and together the pixels form the image.

Photographs taken with phones, screenshots and scanned pages are raster images. Raster graphics are useful for photographs because they can represent gradual changes in light, colour, texture and shadow.

Every raster image has pixel dimensions. An image measuring 1,200 × 800 pixels is 1,200 pixels across and 800 pixels down. It contains 1,200 × 800 = 960,000 pixels. That tells you how many pixels are recorded, not how many centimetres wide the image will appear on paper.

If a small photograph is enlarged too much, its existing pixels are spread over a larger area. It may look blurry or show square blocks. This is pixelation. Enlarging an image does not automatically recover details that were never recorded.

Figure 1. Enlargement can make individual pixel blocks visible in a raster image.

Tumaini example: A clear photograph of students using computers could help people recognise what the workshop is about. However, a tiny photograph copied from a thumbnail may look blurred if stretched across the whole poster.

1.2 Vector graphics: drawings made from shapes

A vector graphic is described through points, lines, curves and shapes rather than a fixed grid of coloured pixels. The software stores instructions about the shapes and redraws them when the graphic changes size. That is why a vector logo normally stays sharp when enlarged.

Imagine drawing a small circle and a large circle with a compass. The size changes, but the rule for drawing a circle stays the same. Vector artwork works more like such instructions than like a mosaic of coloured squares.

Vectors are well suited to simple logos, arrows, icons and diagrams. They are not automatically better for photographs, because a photograph contains complex textures and many small colour changes. One publication can contain both types.

Tumaini example: Use a raster photograph to show students at computers, and a vector book or lightbulb symbol for the learning centre's logo. Each graphic has a different job.

Remember: You do not need to choose only raster or only vector for a whole publication. Select the type that suits each visual element.

1.3 File formats: JPEG, PNG, SVG and PDF

A file format determines how information is stored. The letters after the last dot in a filename form its extension. For example, students.jpg is a JPEG file. The extension provides a useful clue about the format, but it does not guarantee that the image is high quality.

FormatTypical purposeImportant point
JPEG (.jpg)Photographs with many coloursUsually creates smaller files by discarding some detail; no transparency
PNG (.png)Screenshots, clear graphic edges, transparent backgroundsUses lossless image compression; can be larger for photographs
SVG (.svg)Scalable logos and shape-based illustrationsUsually vector-based, although some SVGs contain raster images
PDF (.pdf)Sharing and printing a completed publicationA document format that may contain text, raster images and vector graphics

Transparency means that an image can have areas through which the background behind it is visible. A transparent logo can sit on a coloured poster without a white rectangle around it. JPEG does not provide this type of transparency, while PNG and suitable SVG artwork can.

Compression reduces the storage space used by a file. Lossy compression can remove some picture information to reduce size; JPEG commonly uses it. Lossless compression preserves the stored image information, as PNG does. Image processing and compression will be studied in greater detail in the final lecture.

Also distinguish between an editable working file and a final exported file. You may need the original design file to change a heading later, even if you have already shared the finished notice as a PDF.

1.4 Cropping and resizing are not the same

Cropping removes unwanted areas around the edges of an image. Cropping a photograph of three students to focus on one student changes which part of the scene is visible. Resizing changes the image's dimensions or the size at which it is displayed. A picture can be resized without cutting away any part of the scene.

Do not confuse pixel dimensions with file size. Dimensions such as 1,200 × 800 describe pixels across and down. File size, often measured in kilobytes (KB) or megabytes (MB), describes the storage the file uses. Two 1,200 × 800 images may have different file sizes because they use different formats or compression settings.

Once you have chosen a suitable image, the next task is to organise the words so that readers can find the workshop details quickly. This is where typography becomes important.

2. Typography: organising words for readers

Typography is the selection and arrangement of text to make a message clear, readable and visually organised. It involves more than choosing a decorative font: it includes text size, boldness, spacing, alignment and the order in which information attracts attention.

2.1 Typeface, font, serif and sans-serif

A typeface is a family of letter designs, such as Arial or Georgia. A font is a particular variation used on the page, for example Arial Bold at 18 points. In everyday use, people sometimes call both simply a font.

A serif typeface such as Georgia has small finishing strokes on some letters. A sans-serif typeface such as Arial has simpler letter ends. Neither is always the best choice; readability, audience and consistency matter more than choosing an unusual style.

Font size affects how large letters appear. Font weight describes how light or bold they look. Bold text is useful for emphasis, but if every line is bold, nothing stands out. Beginners can often achieve a good result by using one readable typeface family with two or three clear sizes.

2.2 Visual hierarchy: what should appear first?

Visual hierarchy is the arrangement of information according to importance. In an event notice, readers should quickly recognise the main title before moving to the explanation, date, time and venue.

Example: text arranged in reading order

BASIC COMPUTER SKILLS WORKSHOP

Learn to create documents and organise files

When: Saturday, 10:00 a.m.

Where: Community Hall

The title is large and bold because it identifies the event. The short sentence tells readers what they will learn. The time and venue are smaller but clearly labelled and grouped together. Good typography allows the reader to understand the basic message in a few seconds.

Figure 2. Different text sizes and positions create a clear reading order.

2.3 Alignment, line spacing and white space

Alignment means arranging elements along a common edge or line. For example, placing several lines of text along the same left edge makes them easier to follow. Centred headings can be useful, but centring long paragraphs often makes reading more difficult.

Line spacing is the distance between lines. If lines are too close, the words look crowded; if they are too far apart, one paragraph may appear disconnected. White space is the unoccupied area around text and graphics. It does not have to be white: its purpose is to give different pieces of information room and help readers see what belongs together.

For Tumaini's notice, keep the time and venue near each other because they are related. Leave space between the title, photograph and event details. Make sure the text is easy to see against its background; this difference is called contrast.

Check your own design: Can readers identify the event name first? Do they find the time and place easily? Are the headings and body text consistent? If the answer is no, adjust the hierarchy before adding decoration.

Clear text still needs appropriate colour. We now consider colour as a way to support—not replace—the reading order.

3. Working with colour

Colour attracts attention, separates related information and helps a publication look consistent. It is most effective when there is a reason for using it. A notice with many unrelated bright colours may catch the eye but still be difficult to understand.

3.1 Hue, saturation and lightness

Hue is the basic colour family, such as red, green or blue. Saturation describes how vivid or muted a colour appears. Bright orange is highly saturated, while a dull grey-orange is less saturated. Lightness describes how light or dark a colour is, such as pale blue compared with navy blue.

These ideas matter for contrast. Light yellow writing against a white background is difficult to see because the colours are similar in lightness. Dark navy writing on a pale background is usually easier to read. A visually attractive combination is not useful if students must struggle to read it.

3.2 RGB and CMYK: screen colours and printing colours

Phones, computer screens and televisions display colours using light. A widely used screen colour model is RGB, meaning Red, Green and Blue. Many printing methods combine inks or toner using CMYK: Cyan, Magenta, Yellow and Key (black).

QuestionRGBCMYK
What produces the colour?Light from a screenInk or toner on paper
Common useDigital displaysMany colour printing workflows
Why does it matter?Shows colours to the screen readerHelps prepare and evaluate printed output

A teal heading that looks bright on a phone may appear different on paper. Screens emit light, while paper reflects light. The printer, paper and settings also affect the result. Therefore, check a printed proof when a publication will be printed.

3.3 Building a simple palette

A colour palette is a small, deliberate selection of colours used together. For an educational notice, you might choose a pale background, dark navy body text and teal headings. Orange could highlight one important item, such as a date, but it should not compete with every sentence.

Do not depend on colour alone to communicate meaning. Instead of only making a deadline red, write Registration closes today as well. This helps readers with colour-vision differences and people reading a black-and-white printout.

We now have three connected choices: a suitable picture or symbol, clearly organised words and a restrained palette. Let us apply all three to the same notice.

4. Worked example: improving the Tumaini notice

Imagine that the first notice contains a tiny stretched photograph, five different fonts, an event time far away from the venue and bright colours behind every line. The details may be technically present, but readers have difficulty finding them.

Figure 3. Examine the notice and consider which details are difficult to find or read.

Begin the improvement with the message, not an effect. Make Basic Computer Skills Workshop a clear heading. Place the supporting details underneath and keep the time and venue together. Use a good-quality raster photograph if one is relevant, alongside a small scalable logo. Use one readable typeface family, leave space between sections and choose a small colour palette with adequate contrast.

ElementProblemBetter choice and reason
ImageA tiny photograph is enlarged until blurryUse a clearer raster photo at an appropriate size; keep the logo crisp
WordsTitle and supporting details look equally importantGive the title greater size and group time with venue
TypefaceMany unrelated styles competeUse one family with consistent heading and body variations
ColourBright text on bright backgroundsUse a pale background, dark text and limited accents

Notice that none of these improvements requires an advanced software command. They require thoughtful decisions about what the reader should understand.

5. Check your understanding

Try these short situations before moving to the longer review questions. Give a reason for each answer, not just a one-word label.

  1. A college logo becomes blurry on a large poster. Which type of graphic would normally be a better starting point for a simple logo?
  2. You remove empty space from the side of a photograph. Did you crop or resize it?
  3. A workshop notice contains the correct time, but no one notices it. What could be changed in the typography or layout?
  4. A notice must appear on phones and also be printed. Why should the colours be checked in both settings?
